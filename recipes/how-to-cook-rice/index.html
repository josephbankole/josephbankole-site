How to cook rice

For long grain white rice, use 2 cups of water to 1 cup of rice and simmer covered for 15 to 18 minutes. Basmati wants about 1.5 to 1, jasmine about 1.25 to 1, short grain white about 1.25 to 1, and brown rice about 2.25 to 1 with 40 to 45 minutes of cooking. Rinse white rice until the water runs nearly clear to wash off loose surface starch that would otherwise glue the grains together. Then leave the pan covered and off the heat for 10 minutes before you lift the lid. That rest is where the moisture evens out, and skipping it is the most common reason rice is wet at the bottom and dry on top.

Joseph Bankole30 August 20267 min read

  • Prep 5 min
  • Cook 18 min
  • Makes 4, about 3 cups cooked from 1 cup dry

Ingredients

  • 1 cup long grain white rice
  • 2 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon oil or butter, optional

Equipment. Saucepan with a tight fitting lid, Fine mesh sieve, Clean tea towel, Fork

Method

  1. Put the rice in a sieve and rinse under cold water, stirring it with your hand, until the water running off is only faintly cloudy. Two or three rinses is usually enough. Drain well.
  2. Tip the rice into the saucepan with the water and salt.
  3. Bring it to the boil uncovered, then turn the heat to the lowest setting the hob will hold and put the lid on.
  4. Simmer for 15 to 18 minutes for long grain white, 12 minutes for jasmine, 40 to 45 minutes for brown. Do not lift the lid to check.
  5. Take the pan off the heat. Lay a clean tea towel across the top of the pan, put the lid back on over it, and leave it for 10 minutes. The towel absorbs the condensation that would otherwise drip back onto the rice.
  6. Lift the lid and fluff with a fork, lifting from the bottom rather than stirring, so the steam escapes and the grains separate.

How much water per cup of rice, by type?

These are the USA Rice Federation's figures for the absorption method, which are the closest thing to a standard reference, with the ratio expressed as parts liquid to one part rice by volume.

White long grain: 2 to 1, 15 to 18 minutes.

White medium grain: 1.5 to 1, 15 to 18 minutes.

White short grain: 1.25 to 1, 15 to 18 minutes.

Brown, medium or long grain: 2.25 to 1, 40 to 45 minutes.

Parboiled white: 2.25 to 1, 20 minutes. Parboiled brown: 2.25 to 1, 25 minutes.

Arborio: 4 to 1, 20 to 30 minutes, because risotto is a different process and the liquid goes in gradually.

Wild rice: 3 to 1, 40 to 50 minutes.

Aromatic long grains are where the published numbers fall apart. USA Rice lists American grown jasmine and basmati at 2 to 1, but imported Thai jasmine and Indian basmati are drier grains that take considerably less. RecipeTin Eats tested jasmine at 1.25 to 1 and found it fluffy without rinsing at all. Most basmati packets and Indian cooks land around 1.5 to 1. Start at 1.5 to 1 for basmati and 1.25 to 1 for jasmine, then adjust by a tablespoon or two once you know your pan.

One cup of dry rice yields about three cups cooked, which is four modest side portions.

Should you rinse rice, and how many times?

For white rice, yes, unless you want it sticky. America's Test Kitchen's reason is mechanical rather than nutritional: rinsing removes the loose surface starch that would otherwise absorb water during cooking and glue the grains to each other.

Zojirushi, who make rice cookers for a living, go further and rinse three to four times, stirring in circles between changes of water, and say it makes the finished rice fluffier, whiter and more fragrant.

Stop when the water is only faintly cloudy rather than perfectly clear. Chasing clear water means several more changes, and enriched white rice carries its added B vitamins as a surface coating that washes off with them.

Rinsing also removes some inorganic arsenic, which accumulates in rice more than in most grains. The reduction from a cold rinse is real but modest, and it is a side benefit rather than a reason.

There is one case for not rinsing. Short grain rice destined for a paella, a risotto or a rice pudding wants that surface starch, because the starch is what thickens the dish. If you have already rinsed, cut the water by about two tablespoons per cup, since wet rice arrives in the pan carrying some.

Does the finger knuckle method actually work?

It works in a saucepan, for the reason most people get wrong. The knuckle is not measuring a ratio. It is measuring a fixed depth of water above the rice, and that fixed depth is doing a specific job.

The Exploratorium's explanation is the clearest one. Water in the pan does two things: it soaks into the grains, and it evaporates. Absorption scales with the amount of rice, and it happens to be true that pouring water in until the rice is just submerged gets you roughly the right amount for absorption, because the gaps between dry grains hold about as much water as the grains themselves will take up. The extra inch on top is the evaporation allowance, and evaporation depends on the surface area of the pan rather than on how much rice is in it. So the same inch works whether you are cooking one cup or three, in the same pot.

The method, properly stated: rinse and drain the rice, level it in the pan, add water until it just covers the grains, then rest your fingertip on the surface of the rice, not the bottom of the pan, and add water until it reaches the first crease of that finger. That is about an inch, and it is calibrated for a pan around 8 inches across.

It breaks in three situations. A very wide pan evaporates more than an inch of water and you get dry rice. A very narrow deep pan evaporates less and you get wet rice. And brown rice cooks for three times as long, so the evaporation allowance needs to be far larger than an inch.

Zojirushi tell people not to use it at all, and their objection is fair on its own terms: fingers vary, and their machines have water lines calibrated to a specific measuring cup that do the job exactly. In your own pan, on your own hob, with your own hand, the knuckle is a reasonable instrument. In their cooker it is a worse one than the line already printed inside it.

How do you cook rice in a rice cooker?

Use the cup that came with the machine, and ignore the one in your kitchen drawer. A rice cooker cup is 180ml, the Japanese go, which is three quarters of a US cup. The water lines moulded inside the bowl are numbered to that cup and to nothing else. Two of those cups of rice means water to line 2.

Rinse in a sieve rather than in the inner pot if you can, because the non stick coating scratches and a scratched pot cooks unevenly.

Brown rice needs both more water and the brown rice programme, which soaks the grain before it starts heating. Filling to the white rice line and pressing the normal button gives you gravel.

Leave it on keep warm for ten minutes after the cycle ends before you open the lid, then fluff. The rest is the same rest a saucepan needs, and the machine does not do it for you.

Why does rice need to rest off the heat?

Because when the timer goes off, the rice is not evenly cooked. The grains at the bottom sat in liquid water; the ones at the top cooked in steam. Ten minutes with the lid on and the heat off lets the remaining moisture redistribute through the pot, and lets the starch in each grain firm up enough to hold its shape when you disturb it.

America's Test Kitchen adds a refinement worth copying. Take the pan off the heat, lay a clean tea towel over the top, then put the lid back on over the towel and leave it ten minutes. Steam condenses on a cold lid and drips back onto the rice as water; the towel catches it instead.

Fluff with a fork rather than a spoon, and lift rather than stir. USA Rice's instruction is to separate the grains and let the steam out, and a spoon crushes as much as it separates.

This is also the step to skip if you want sticky rice. Leaving it packed and undisturbed does the opposite of everything above.

Why do published ratios disagree so much?

Because most of the water in a published ratio is not for the rice. America's Test Kitchen tested every variety they had and found that all of them cooked properly on a 1 to 1 ratio of rice to water by volume. That is the absorption figure. Everything above it is an allowance for evaporation.

Evaporation is a property of your equipment rather than your rice. A wide pan with a loose lid on a fierce burner loses far more water in eighteen minutes than a narrow pan with a heavy lid on a low flame. Brown rice sits on the heat for two and a half times as long, so it loses two and a half times as much, which is the whole reason its published ratio is higher rather than any thirstiness in the bran.

The practical consequence is that a ratio is a starting point tied to somebody else's pan. Cook the same rice twice in your pan, note whether it came out wet or dry, and move the water by two tablespoons per cup. After that the number is yours and it stops changing.
