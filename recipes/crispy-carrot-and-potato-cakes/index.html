Crispy Carrot and Potato Cakes

Two cups of grated carrot and a cup and a quarter of grated potato, squeezed dry, bound with one beaten egg and a tablespoon of gluten free flour, seasoned, then fried in batches until the edges go dark. Crumbled feta and pitted black olives go on at the end. Sweet from the carrot, salty from the feta, and best straight out of the pan. It makes about 10 cakes, enough for four as a starter, and runs to roughly 20 minutes of grating and mixing plus another 20 at the stove.

Before you cook this.

The mixture contains raw egg, so cook the cakes through and do not taste the raw batter. Shallow frying spits: squeeze the grated vegetables properly dry, keep the oil at a quarter inch rather than deeper, and lay the cakes into the pan away from you.

What goes into it

  • 2 cups of grated carrot
  • 1 1/4 cups of grated peeled potatoes (squeeze water)
  • 1 tablespoon of flour (gluten free)
  • 1 beaten egg
  • 3/4 teaspoon of salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon of black pepper
  • 3/4 cup of crumbled feta
  • 10 pitted black olives
  • 2 cloves of garlic, minced (not in the original notebook, named in step 2 but absent from his list; the quantity is my estimate)
  • Neutral oil for frying, about 1/4 inch in the pan (not in the original notebook, step 3 fries in batches but no oil appears anywhere in his list)

Equipment. Box grater, Tea towel or muslin for squeezing, Heavy frying pan

How to make it

  1. Mix the carrot, potatoes, flour, egg, salt and pepper
  2. Add the minced garlic
  3. Fry in batches
  4. Garnish with feta and olives

What goes into it

Raw grated carrot and raw grated peeled potato, which puts this in the latke and rosti family rather than the mashed-potato-cake family. The binder is one beaten egg and a single tablespoon of flour. Feta and olives are a garnish here, added after frying.

The ten pitted black olives are the best internal evidence for the yield. One per cake reads as ten cakes.

Will one tablespoon of flour hold them together

On the published rates, probably not. Three and a quarter cups of grated vegetable against one tablespoon of flour and one egg is about a third of the usual binder. The rate across the fritter family runs closer to a tablespoon of flour per cup of grated vegetable, so the comparable figure here is 2 to 3 tablespoons.

The flour is also specified gluten free, and a blend without xanthan or a similar gum binds noticeably less than wheat flour, which widens the gap rather than closing it.

Two things work the other way. Hard-squeezed potato releases starch, which binds, and there is 3/4 cup of feta available. Folding the feta into the mixture instead of scattering it on top is the cheapest fix. If the cakes still break up, take the flour to 3 tablespoons.

How to fry them

'Fry in batches' is the whole of the frying instruction, with no heat level, no oil depth and no timing. The figures the comparable recipes agree on: about a quarter inch of neutral oil at 350F, roughly 3 minutes on the first side and 1 to 3 on the second, until deep golden.

Press each cake flat as it goes into the pan, and drain them on paper towel afterwards. Crowding the pan drops the oil temperature and steams the cakes instead of crisping them.

Where this version differs

One tablespoon of flour for 3.25 cups of grated vegetable is roughly a third of the published rate of about a tablespoon per cup, and a gluten free blend binds less than wheat flour. The squeezed potato starch and the 3/4 cup of feta may carry it. If they break up, fold the feta into the mixture or take the flour to 3 tablespoons.

The squeeze instruction sits against the potato only. Two cups of raw grated carrot is the larger volume and holds a lot of water; the comparable recipes squeeze or salt and drain the whole grated mix for 30 minutes.

No oil temperature and no per-side timing are recorded. Consensus is a quarter inch of neutral oil at 350F, about 3 minutes on the first side and 1 to 3 on the second.

Every comparable raw-grated fritter includes grated or finely diced onion. This version has none. Not an error, but it is the most common divergence from the family.

The flour is written as gluten free. The recipe has not been tested or certified as coeliac safe, and the feta and any other packaged ingredient need checking against their own labels.
