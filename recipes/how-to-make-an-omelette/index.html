How to make an omelette

Three eggs want an 8 to 9 inch non stick pan, two eggs a 7 to 8 inch one. Whisk until there are no streaks of white left, about 30 seconds, and season the eggs rather than the finished omelette. Melt butter over low to medium heat until the foam subsides but before it colours, add the eggs, and keep them moving with a fork or a spatula for 45 to 90 seconds. Take the pan off the heat while the surface is still glossy and slightly wet. It carries on setting on the way to the plate, and an omelette that looks done in the pan is overdone by the time you eat it.

Joseph Bankole30 August 20266 min read

  • Prep 3 min
  • Cook 4 min
  • Makes 1

Ingredients

  • 3 large eggs
  • 1/2 tablespoon butter, plus a knob to finish
  • A pinch of fine salt
  • Black pepper
  • Chives, cheese or herbs, if you want them

Equipment. 8 to 9 inch non stick or well seasoned skillet, Fork, chopsticks or a silicone spatula, Warmed plate

Method

  1. Crack the eggs into a bowl, add the salt and pepper, and whisk with a fork for about 30 seconds until the colour is uniform and no ribbons of white remain.
  2. Set the pan over low heat and let it come up slowly. A cold pan rushed over high heat has hot spots; ten minutes on low gets rid of them.
  3. Add the butter. Wait for the foaming to subside and swirl to coat the base. Do not let it brown.
  4. Pour in the eggs. They should sizzle faintly, not crackle.
  5. Stir constantly with a fork or a pair of chopsticks in small circles, scraping cooked egg off the sides as you go, for 45 to 90 seconds. Shake the pan occasionally so the loose egg finds the gaps.
  6. Stop while the top is still glossy and loose. Smooth it into an even layer with a spatula.
  7. Scatter over any filling, turn the heat off, and either cover the pan for 1 to 2 minutes or roll the omelette straight away from the handle side.
  8. Tip it onto a warmed plate, seam side down, and rub the top with a knob of butter.

What size pan do you need for how many eggs?

Roughly one inch of pan diameter per egg, plus five. Two eggs suit a 7 to 8 inch pan, three eggs an 8 to 9 inch, four eggs a 10 inch, and six eggs a 12 inch. America's Test Kitchen uses an 8 inch non stick skillet for its French omelette and a 10 inch for a larger folded American style one.

Get it wrong in either direction and you feel it immediately. Too big and the eggs spread into a thin sheet that sets before you can move it, which is the fastest route to a rubbery omelette. Too small and the layer is so deep that the base overcooks while the top is still liquid, and folding it becomes a wrestling match.

Non stick is not snobbery here. An omelette is a thin protein film released in one piece; a stainless pan can do it, but it needs more fat and a better hand.

How hot should the pan actually be?

Lower than most people run it, and preheated for longer. Jacques Pepin's method has the butter melting over low heat and never browning, because the moment butter colours the pan is already hotter than the eggs want.

America's Test Kitchen preheats the empty skillet over low heat for ten minutes before the eggs go anywhere near it. That is not about reaching a high temperature, it is about reaching an even one. A pan blasted for ninety seconds has a scorching ring where the burner sits and cool edges, and the omelette records both.

There is a case for a brief hot moment. The British Egg Information Service starts the pan hot to set the base fast, then drops to medium for the rest. That builds a bit more structure and a paler skin, which suits a folded omelette with a filling. For a rolled French omelette with a creamy middle, keep it low throughout.

The test is audible. The eggs should whisper when they hit the pan. If they crackle, the pan is too hot and you will get brown patches and squeaky curds.

How long do you whisk, and should you add milk?

About 30 seconds, until the mixture is one colour and you cannot see strands of white. Under whisked eggs cook into a marbled omelette with rubbery white streaks running through it, because the white sets several degrees before the yolk does.

There is no need to beat air into it. The British Egg Information Service only calls for a proper froth when you are deliberately making a fluffy omelette; for a standard one, a fork and half a minute is the whole job.

Milk is a matter of taste rather than physics, and cooks disagree. A splash of water or milk dilutes the protein network slightly and makes a softer curd, but it also turns to steam in the pan and can leave a wet, spongy omelette. If you want a richer texture, cold butter is the better lever: America's Test Kitchen cubes a tablespoon of butter and freezes it while the pan preheats, then stirs it through the raw eggs so it disperses without melting.

Season the eggs in the bowl, not the omelette on the plate. Salt needs to be dissolved through the mixture to do anything useful.

Butter or oil?

Butter, for flavour, with the caveat that its milk solids brown at a lower temperature than most oils. That is fine if you keep the heat where it should be, and a problem if you do not.

Clarified butter or ghee removes the caveat. The milk proteins that scorch have already been taken out, so the smoke point is much higher and you can be less careful. It tastes more concentrated too.

A mixture of a neutral oil and a knob of butter is the practical compromise most home cooks use, and it is what the British Egg Information Service suggests for a fluffy omelette. The oil raises the temperature at which the solids burn; the butter still does the flavouring.

Half a tablespoon is enough for three eggs in a properly non stick pan. Excess fat pools at the edges and fries the rim while the middle steams.

When do you take it out of the pan?

While it still looks slightly underdone. Eggs carry on cooking after the heat goes off, and a set omelette in the pan is a dry omelette on the plate.

America's Test Kitchen makes this explicit in its method: turn the heat off while the eggs are still runny, smooth them into an even layer, cover the pan, and let residual heat finish the job. One minute covered gives a runnier omelette, two minutes a firmer one, and neither toughens the eggs the way another minute on the burner would.

Pepin's version does not even use the lid. The pan comes off the heat while the surface is wet and glossy, and the rolling itself, plus the trip to the plate, is the last of the cooking.

ThermoWorks has measured carryover on larger cuts and the principle is the same: heat already in the food keeps moving inward after you stop applying more. An omelette is thin, so the effect is over in under a minute, but a minute is the entire difference between creamy and squeaky.

Why did my omelette come out rubbery?

Almost always heat, and almost always too much of it. Egg proteins set gently between about 145F and 160F. Push past that and they contract, squeeze out the water they were holding, and go from a soft gel to a tight, weeping curd. That water is what you see pooling on the plate.

The second cause is a pan that is too large for the egg count, which makes the layer thin enough to blow through the window between set and overset before you can react.

The third is leaving it in the pan while you assemble the plate, find the cheese, or answer the door. Once the eggs are set, the pan is still hotter than they are.

If it does happen, there is no rescuing it. Turn the heat down a notch, use a smaller pan, and stop a good thirty seconds earlier next time.
