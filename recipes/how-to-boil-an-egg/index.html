How to boil an egg

Lower large eggs straight from the fridge into already boiling water, or into a steamer basket over an inch of boiling water, and count six minutes for a runny yolk, nine for a jammy one and twelve to thirteen for a firm one. Move them into iced water the moment the timer goes. A cold water start also works and cracks fewer shells, but the eggs peel worse and the clock starts later because the pan has to come up to heat first. The grey ring around an overcooked yolk is ferrous sulphide, and it means the egg was held too long or too hot.

Joseph Bankole30 August 20266 min read

  • Cook 13 min
  • Makes 4 eggs

Ingredients

  • Large eggs, cold from the fridge
  • Water
  • Ice, for the ice bath

Equipment. Saucepan with a tight lid, Steamer basket (optional), Slotted spoon or spider skimmer, Timer, Bowl for the ice bath

Method

  1. Fill a saucepan with about an inch of water and bring it to a rolling boil. Use a steamer basket if you have one; the eggs do not need to sit in the water.
  2. Lower the eggs in one at a time with a slotted spoon or a spider. Do not tip them in from the carton.
  3. Cover, drop the heat to medium low, and start the timer. Six minutes for a runny yolk, nine for jammy, twelve to thirteen for firm all the way through.
  4. While they cook, half fill a bowl with ice and cold water.
  5. Lift the eggs out at the timer and put them straight into the ice bath.
  6. Leave them there for at least ten minutes, longer if you want them cold for a salad.
  7. Tap each egg all over on the worktop, roll it under the palm to craze the shell, then peel from the blunt end where the air cell sits, under running water.

How long do you boil an egg for soft, medium or hard?

Times below are for large eggs taken cold from the fridge and lowered into water already at a rolling boil, or into a steamer basket over an inch of boiling water. The two methods run within about a minute of each other.

6 minutes: white just set, yolk fully liquid. The dipping egg.

7 minutes: white firm, yolk still runs but holds its shape when the egg is halved.

9 minutes: jammy. Yolk set at the edge, fudgy in the middle. Ramen eggs live here.

11 minutes: yolk set through but still bright and slightly moist.

12 to 13 minutes: firm and crumbly all the way through. This is where America's Test Kitchen lands for hard cooked, at 13 minutes steamed or 13 minutes in boiling water.

Past about 14 minutes you are only making the yolk chalkier and encouraging the grey ring. There is no benefit to it.

Medium eggs come out about a minute quicker and extra large about a minute slower. If you are cooking more than four or five at once, add thirty seconds, because the eggs pull heat out of the pan as they go in.

Should you start eggs in cold water or in boiling water?

Boiling water start, or steam, if you care about peeling. America's Test Kitchen puts it in one sentence: starting the eggs in hot rather than cold water makes the white's proteins seize and bond together, so they stop sticking to the shell membrane. The timing is also honest, because the clock starts when the egg meets the heat rather than whenever your pan happens to reach a boil.

The cold water start is the American Egg Board method and it still works. Cover the eggs by an inch of cold water, bring it just to the boil, take the pan off the heat, cover it, and leave large eggs to stand for about 12 minutes, medium for 9, extra large for 15. Nothing ever comes to a violent boil, so fewer shells crack and the yolks are less likely to go grey. The trade is that the eggs peel worse and the total time depends on your hob and your pan.

Steam is the compromise most test kitchens have landed on. The eggs never take a rolling boil in the face, the water does not drop in temperature when they go in, and they peel like the hot water start.

How do you stop eggs cracking in the pan?

Two things crack a shell: the thermal shock of a cold egg hitting hot water, and the air cell at the blunt end expanding faster than the air can escape through the shell.

Lowering the eggs rather than dropping them is the single biggest fix, and it costs nothing. Use a slotted spoon or a spider, one egg at a time, and set them on the bottom rather than letting them land. Keep them in one layer so they are not knocking against each other in the boil.

Steaming removes most of the rest of the problem, because the eggs sit above the water instead of being churned around in it. ThermoWorks makes the point directly: a boiling water start is where the cracking happens, and steaming sidesteps it.

Turning the heat down to a bare simmer once the eggs are in helps too. A rolling boil does not cook an egg any faster than a gentle one, it just rattles it against the pan.

Pricking the blunt end with a pin is the old advice and it does very little. If a shell does split, the white leaks out in a ribbon and sets; the egg is still edible, just untidy.

What is the grey ring around the yolk and how do you avoid it?

It is ferrous sulphide. Sulphur in the white and iron in the yolk meet at the boundary between them and react, leaving a grey green film on the outside of the yolk. Both the University of Nebraska and the University of Illinois food science department describe the same reaction, and both name overcooking as the cause.

Two things drive it. Heat and time, mostly, but also the pH of the white. As an egg ages the white becomes more alkaline, and the more alkaline it is the faster the sulphur reacts, so older eggs go grey sooner at the same timing.

The fix is not exotic. Do not cook past the point the yolk is set, and cool the egg fast when you take it out. Illinois notes that a very high iron content in the cooking water can contribute as well, which is worth knowing if you are on a well.

It is harmless. The compound is the same form of iron used in supplements. It just tastes faintly sulphurous and looks poor on a plate.

Do you actually need an ice bath?

For anything you want firm and not grey, yes. An egg lifted out of a pan carries enough stored heat to keep cooking for several minutes, and the ferrous sulphide reaction runs the whole time. Dropping it into iced water stops the clock.

ThermoWorks puts the number at 10 to 15 minutes in the ice bath. America's Test Kitchen calls for 15 minutes after steaming. Five minutes is enough to halt the cooking; the longer soak is about getting the egg cold enough to peel cleanly and slice without smearing.

For a soft boiled egg you are eating straight away, a shorter dunk of about 30 seconds under cold running water is enough. You want the cooking arrested, not the egg chilled.

It also makes peeling easier. Cooling shrinks the egg slightly inside its shell, which is why the American Egg Board tells you to peel while cool rather than hot.

Why are some eggs impossible to peel?

Freshness, mostly, and how the white was cooked. A very fresh egg has a lower pH white that bonds tightly to the inner shell membrane. As the egg sits and loses carbon dioxide through the shell, the white becomes more alkaline and that bond weakens, which is why week old eggs peel better than yesterday's.

The cooking method does the rest. A hot start sets the outer layer of white fast, before it has a chance to adhere. A slow warm up gives it every opportunity.

If you have only fresh eggs, steam them, chill them hard, and crack the shell all over before you start rather than picking at one end. Peeling under running water floats the shards away from the membrane instead of dragging them through the white.
