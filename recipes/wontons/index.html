Wontons

Wontons filled with either shrimp or minced pork, seasoned with sesame oil, soy, ginger, rice wine and white pepper, then steamed for 6 to 8 minutes or fried until golden. Three quarters of a pound of filling wraps 36 to 40 square wrappers, which is one standard 12 oz pack with a few spare. Mixing and wrapping takes about 35 minutes and each steamer batch is done inside ten. Minced pork has to reach 71C / 160F, so the steamed route is the one to trust here; the fried route in the notebook has no oil temperature written against it.

Joseph Bankole30 August 20264 min read

The page offers shrimp or minced pork on one line as though they were interchangeable. On timing and on temperature they are not, and the notes below split them.

Before you cook this.

Minced pork has to reach 71C / 160F. USDA FSIS and Health Canada agree on the number, and ground pork did not get the reduction to 145F that whole-muscle pork cuts did, because grinding spreads surface bacteria through the whole mass. Steaming for 6 to 8 minutes gets a wonton holding one to two teaspoons of filling there. Check one from the middle of the basket with a thermometer before serving the batch, and add two minutes from frozen.

The fried option carries no oil temperature and uses colour as its only doneness cue, which is the dangerous combination with raw pork: a wrapper goes golden long before the filling inside it is safe. His own fried chicken and chicken wings pages both state an oil temperature, so this is an omission rather than a preference. Fry at 350F / 175C, keep the fill to one or two teaspoons, and check 71C / 160F on the first one out of the pan.

Shrimp are the safer of the two fillings. They are done at 63C / 145F and opaque, and they will be cooked well inside either stated time. Keep the filling in the fridge while you wrap a batch of forty, and do not leave the tray sitting out between batches.

What goes into it

  • 3/4 of a pound of shrimp or minced pork
  • 2 cloves of garlic
  • 1 teaspoon each of sesame oil, soy sauce, salt, grated ginger, sugar, rice wine, white pepper
  • 36 to 40 square wonton wrappers, one 12 oz pack (not in the original notebook, named in step 3 but absent from his list, so the page cannot be shopped from as written)
  • Oil for frying, if you fry them rather than steam (not in the original notebook, step 4 offers a fry but no fat appears anywhere in his list)

Equipment. Steamer basket lined with baking paper, Instant-read thermometer

How to make it

  1. Blend shrimp if required
  2. Mix protein with seasoning
  3. Fill wonton wrappers
  4. Fry for 5 mins until golden brown, or steam for 6-8 minutes

What goes into it

Three quarters of a pound of protein, two cloves of garlic, and a teaspoon each of seven seasonings: sesame oil, soy sauce, salt, grated ginger, sugar, rice wine and white pepper. That is a lean filling by the standards of most published versions, which is worth knowing before you mix it.

The wrappers are the one thing the page cannot be shopped from. They appear in step 3 and nowhere in the ingredient list, so they are promoted into the list above.

How many wrappers do you need?

Thirty six to forty. His seven seasonings are almost all liquid at a teaspoon each, so the finished filling barely moves off the 340 g of protein he started with. At the usual one to two teaspoons per wrapper that is about 34 wontons, and 36 to 40 allows for a slightly more generous hand.

One standard 12 oz supermarket pack holds roughly 50 square wrappers, so a single pack covers the batch with spares. Fill more heavily than two teaspoons and two things happen: the wrapper will not seal, and the extra mass of raw pork will not come up to temperature inside the stated steaming time.

Should you steam them or fry them?

Steam them. Six to eight minutes is corroborated for a wonton holding one to two teaspoons of filling, and it sits above the boiled equivalent, which published sources put at three to five minutes. Boiling moves heat faster than steaming does, so the longer steaming figure is the conservative one.

The fry as written is the weaker half of the page and the notes below say why. Whichever route you take, rest the wrapped wontons on a floured or lined tray in a single layer. Stacked, they glue to each other and tear on the way into the pan.

Where this version differs

Most published pork fillings carry an egg as a binder, two stalks of finely chopped green onion, and a tablespoon of oyster sauce. His has none of the three. The egg is the one a cook feels first, because a filling seasoned only with liquids is loose and crumbles out of the fold.

The page also says only "Fill wonton wrappers" and stops there. Published methods moisten the edges with a fingertip of water, fold to a triangle, then moisten and press the two bottom corners together. Skip the seal and fried wontons burst in the oil while steamed ones open and dump their filling. No boiling option is given either, though it is the most common domestic method.

Where this version differs

"Until they float" is a wrapper hydration signal rather than a doneness signal. It correlates acceptably for small wontons and it is widely used, but it is not the safety check and should not stand in for one.
