How to make pizza dough

Four bases from 500g of strong white bread flour, 325g of water, 10g of salt and 2g of instant yeast, which is 65 per cent hydration. Mix, rest for 20 minutes, then knead for 8 to 10 minutes by hand or 5 minutes in a stand mixer. Prove at around 21C / 70F for 1 to 2 hours, divide into four 210g balls, and either use them after another hour or refrigerate them for 24 to 72 hours. The cold ferment is worth doing. Yeast activity drops away in the fridge while the flour's own enzymes keep working, so the dough gains flavour without over-rising.

Joseph Bankole30 August 20269 min read

  • Prep 30 min
  • Makes 4 balls of about 210g, for four 10 inch pizzas

Ingredients

  • 500g (4 cups plus 3 tablespoons) strong white bread flour
  • 325g (1 cup plus 6 tablespoons) water, at about 20C / 68F
  • 10g (1 3/4 teaspoons) fine sea salt
  • 2g (2/3 teaspoon) instant dried yeast for a cold ferment, or 5g (1 1/2 teaspoons) for same day
  • 15g (1 tablespoon) olive oil, optional (not in the original notebook, not needed for a Neapolitan style base, but it softens the crumb and helps a home oven base brown, so it is listed as a choice rather than assumed)
  • Semolina or fine polenta, for the peel or the tray (not in the original notebook, used when shaping and launching, and every dough recipe assumes you already have some)

Equipment. Digital scale, Large mixing bowl, Stand mixer with a dough hook, optional, Dough scraper, Lidded containers or a tray with clingfilm, for proving the balls, Baking steel, stone or a heavy upturned tray

Method

  1. Weigh the flour into the bowl and stir the salt through it. If you are using instant yeast, stir that through too; it does not need dissolving.
  2. Pour in the water and mix with a hand or a spoon until there is no dry flour left and you have a shaggy mass. Add the olive oil now if you are using it.
  3. Cover the bowl and leave it for 20 minutes. The flour hydrates on its own during this rest and the kneading that follows takes noticeably less effort.
  4. Knead for 8 to 10 minutes by hand on a bare worktop, or 5 minutes on medium in a stand mixer with the dough hook. Stop when the dough is smooth, springy, and pulls back when you stretch it.
  5. Check it with the windowpane test: pull a piece the size of a walnut and stretch it between your fingers. If it thins enough to see light through without tearing, the gluten is developed.
  6. Round the dough into a ball, put it in a lightly oiled bowl, cover, and leave it at around 21C / 70F for 1 to 2 hours, until it is noticeably puffy and about half again its size.
  7. Tip it out, divide into four pieces of about 210g each, and shape each into a tight ball by folding the edges into the middle and rolling it seam side down under a cupped hand.
  8. For same day pizza: cover the balls and prove for another 1 to 2 hours until slack and pillowy. For a cold ferment: put each ball in its own lidded container, refrigerate for 24 to 72 hours, then take them out 2 to 3 hours before baking.
  9. Shape by pressing from the centre outwards on a semolina dusted surface, leaving the outer inch untouched. Do not use a rolling pin; it flattens the gas you have spent two days making.
  10. Bake as hot as your oven goes, on a steel or stone preheated for at least 45 minutes.

What goes into pizza dough, in grams and in cups?

Flour, water, salt and yeast. Oil and sugar are optional and neither belongs in a Neapolitan dough.

500g flour, which is 4 cups plus 3 tablespoons of strong white bread flour by King Arthur's standard of 120g to the cup.

325g water, which is 1 cup plus 6 tablespoons.

10g fine sea salt, about 1 3/4 teaspoons. That is 2 per cent of the flour weight, which is where most pizza doughs sit.

2g instant dried yeast, about 2/3 of a teaspoon, for a cold ferment. Use 5g, about 1 1/2 teaspoons, if you want to bake the same day.

15g olive oil, 1 tablespoon, if you want it.

Weigh it if you can. Cup measurements for flour vary by up to 20 per cent depending on whether the flour was scooped or spooned, and 20 per cent is the difference between a dough at 65 per cent hydration and one at 78. Every serious pizza recipe is written in weights for that reason, and every set of cup equivalents on a page like this is a conversion of the weights rather than the other way round.

The AVPN, who certify Neapolitan pizza, write the formula the same way: one litre of water, 50 to 55g of sea salt, 3g of fresh yeast, and flour added gradually to a total of about 1.8kg.

What hydration should pizza dough be?

Hydration is the weight of the water as a percentage of the weight of the flour. 325g of water into 500g of flour is 65 per cent. It is the single number that tells you most about how a dough will behave.

Published doughs sit anywhere from 55 to 75 per cent and they are all defensible. The AVPN's Neapolitan formula works out at about 55 to 62 per cent. King Arthur's standard pizza crust runs 55 to 71 per cent depending on the season, using less water in a humid summer and more in a dry winter. America's Test Kitchen's hand kneaded New York style dough is up around 73 per cent.

65 per cent is the useful middle for a domestic oven. Below about 60 the dough is stiff, easy to handle and bakes into a denser, crisper base that suits a long slow bake. Above about 70 it is slack and sticky, needs a folding technique rather than kneading, and rewards the very high heat of a wood or gas oven, where the extra water flashes to steam and blows the rim open.

Flour matters as much as the number. A strong bread flour at 12 to 13 per cent protein will take 65 per cent water and stay manageable. A soft plain flour at 9 per cent will not, and the same dough will be a puddle.

If your kitchen is dry or your flour is old, hold back 20g of the water and add it during mixing. It is far easier to add water to a stiff dough than flour to a wet one.

Do you need to activate the yeast first?

Not with instant yeast. It is milled finer and dried differently from active dry yeast, and it is designed to be stirred straight into the flour. This recipe assumes instant.

Active dry yeast does want waking up. King Arthur's method is to dissolve it in about 3 tablespoons of the recipe water warmed to 110F, leave it 10 to 15 minutes until it foams, then add it with the rest of the cool water and the flour.

The temperature range matters more than people think. 105 to 110F is the target for proofing active dry yeast. America's Test Kitchen tested water at 130F and found the doughs came out denser, shorter and slightly sour, and yeast dies outright somewhere in the 130 to 140F band. They also found no time saved by going hotter, because the dough cools within two hours and the control batch catches up anyway.

For this dough, use water at room temperature or a little below. A cold ferment does not want a fast start, and a dough that is too warm going into the fridge takes hours to cool down and over-proves on the way.

If you are not sure your yeast is still alive, proof it whatever type it is. Warm water, a pinch of sugar, ten minutes. If nothing foams, the yeast is dead and no amount of proving time will fix it.

How long do you knead pizza dough?

8 to 10 minutes by hand, or 4 to 7 minutes in a stand mixer with a dough hook. King Arthur's basic pizza crust calls for 4 to 5 minutes on second speed; their enriched version with semolina goes to 7 minutes on the hook. America's Test Kitchen kneads by hand for 7 to 8 minutes.

Give the dough a 20 minute rest between mixing and kneading. The flour absorbs water and starts forming gluten on its own during that rest, so the kneading you then do is shorter and easier. It is the single cheapest improvement available.

Judge it by the windowpane test rather than the clock. Stretch a small piece thin between your fingers; if you can see light through it before it tears, the gluten network is developed. If it tears at once, knead for another two minutes and test again.

One caveat, and it is a real one. The windowpane test is far less relevant for a long fermented dough. Gluten keeps developing biochemically during fermentation, so a dough that will sit in the fridge for 48 hours does not need to pass the test on day one. If you are cold fermenting, knead to smooth and springy and stop worrying about it.

How warm and how long should the dough prove?

Around 21C / 70F for the bulk prove, for 1 to 2 hours, until the dough is noticeably puffy rather than doubled. Most kitchens sit between 18 and 24C, which is fine; the dough simply moves faster at the top of that range.

Yeast works roughly twice as fast for every 8 to 10C you add, which is why proving times in recipes are so unreliable. Watch the dough rather than the clock. A well proved bulk has domed slightly, feels alive and slack when you press it, and holds a shallow dent for a second before easing back.

After dividing and balling, the balls need their own prove. Same day, that is another 1 to 2 hours at room temperature until they have spread slightly and feel pillowy. Underproved balls fight you when you try to open them and spring back into a small thick disc.

Do not prove pizza dough in a warm oven or an airing cupboard. Speed is not the aim. A fast warm prove gives you volume and almost no flavour, and the dough goes from underproved to collapsing in about twenty minutes.

Same day or cold ferment overnight?

Cold ferment if you can plan two days ahead. Same day works and there is nothing wrong with it, but the difference in taste is not subtle.

The mechanism is a split between two clocks. In the fridge at around 4C / 39F, yeast activity falls away almost completely, while the enzymes that came with the flour slow down far less. Amylases keep breaking starch into simple sugars and proteases keep cutting long gluten proteins into shorter chains and free amino acids. Those sugars and amino acids are what brown and what taste of something, and a cold ferment buys you far more enzyme time per unit of yeast activity than a warm one.

Modernist Cuisine put the useful window at 24 to 96 hours and recommend around 4C / 39F, with 24 hours for thin crust styles and 48 for Neapolitan and New York doughs. Below 24 hours you have mostly just chilled the dough. Past about 72 hours the proteases have gone too far and the dough starts tearing when you stretch it.

The practical schedule: mix, bulk prove 1 to 2 hours at room temperature so the yeast gets going, ball, then straight into individual lidded containers in the fridge. Take them out 2 to 3 hours before you bake so they come back to room temperature, which is when they become stretchable.

Cut the yeast for a cold ferment. 2g of instant yeast for 500g of flour is plenty over 48 hours; the 5g you would use for a same day dough will have the balls climbing out of their containers by morning. King Arthur's own long ferment dough uses 1/8 teaspoon of yeast to 250g of flour, which is a fraction of a same day quantity.

Same day is the right choice when you decided at four o'clock that you wanted pizza. Use 5g of yeast, prove for 1 to 2 hours, ball, prove for another hour, and accept a plainer, breadier flavour. Adding a tablespoon of olive oil helps a same day dough, because it makes the crumb softer and covers for the flavour the fermentation has not had time to build.
