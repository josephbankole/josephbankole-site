How to melt chocolate

Chop the chocolate small, put it in a dry bowl, and microwave in 20 to 30 second bursts at half power, stirring thoroughly between each one. Stop while a few lumps are still visible and let the heat already in the bowl finish the job. Dark chocolate should not go past about 50C / 122F, and milk and white chocolate not past about 45C / 113F, because their milk solids scorch sooner. Chocolate seizes into a stiff grainy paste for two reasons: a stray drop of water, or too much heat. Both are recoverable if you are willing to turn the result into a sauce or a ganache rather than a coating.

Joseph Bankole30 August 20266 min read

  • Prep 5 min
  • Cook 2 min

Ingredients

  • 200g chocolate, chopped small or in callets
  • Double cream, milk or boiling water, only if you need to rescue a seized batch

Equipment. Dry microwave safe bowl, glass or ceramic, Silicone spatula, Digital thermometer, Saucepan and a heatproof bowl, for the bain-marie route, Serrated knife or a chocolate chipper

Method

  1. Chop the chocolate to roughly the size of a pea. Uneven pieces melt at different rates and the small ones scorch while the big ones sit there solid.
  2. Check that the bowl and the spatula are bone dry. Wipe them if you are not sure.
  3. Microwave at 50 per cent power for 20 to 30 seconds.
  4. Take the bowl out and stir for a good ten seconds, scraping the sides and the base. Most of the melting happens during the stirring, not during the heating.
  5. Repeat in 20 to 30 second bursts, stirring fully each time, and shorten the bursts to 10 seconds once the mixture is mostly liquid.
  6. Stop when about a fifth of the chocolate is still lumpy. Keep stirring off the heat until it is smooth.
  7. Check the temperature if you have a thermometer. Dark should be under 50C / 122F, milk and white under 45C / 113F, and nothing should ever approach 54C / 130F.

How do you melt chocolate in the microwave without burning it?

Short bursts, half power, and a real stir between every one. Ghirardelli's instruction is 30 second intervals at 50 per cent power, removing and stirring each time. King Arthur tested the same approach at full power in 30 second bursts and got a smooth result in about 90 seconds for three ounces of chopped bittersweet.

Half power is the safer default because a microwave heats unevenly. The chocolate at the edge of the bowl can be well past scorching while the middle is still solid, and you will not know until you stir and find gritty specks that will not disperse.

Stop early. When roughly 80 per cent of the chocolate has melted, take it out and stir until the rest goes. The bowl and the liquid chocolate carry enough stored heat to finish the job, and that residual heat is gentler than another burst.

White chocolate needs shorter bursts than dark, 20 seconds rather than 30, because it has no cocoa solids and a great deal of milk powder and sugar, both of which catch quickly.

Why does chocolate seize, and can you rescue it?

Chocolate is cocoa solids and sugar suspended in cocoa butter. Water breaks that suspension. King Arthur describes the result precisely: the sugar crystals and cocoa particles pull away from the cocoa butter, the emulsion breaks, and the chocolate separates into a chunky mass. Even a drop of condensation off a lid can do it, which is why every bowl and spatula has to be dry.

Overheating causes a similar looking mess by a different route. Past about 54C / 130F the fat separates from the solids and the chocolate goes thick, dull and grainy. Ghirardelli's own guidance is to stay below 130F for exactly this reason.

The counterintuitive rescue for seized chocolate is more liquid, not less. A little water is a disaster; enough water dissolves the sugar and restores a workable emulsion. King Arthur gives the minimums to avoid seizing in the first place: one tablespoon of water or other liquid per 2 ounces of 55 to 60 per cent chocolate, one and a half tablespoons per 2 ounces at 60 to 70 per cent, and two tablespoons per 2 ounces for unsweetened.

So: add boiling water, warm cream or milk a teaspoon at a time, stirring hard after each addition, until the mass turns glossy again. What you have then is a chocolate sauce or a thin ganache. It will never set hard, it cannot be tempered, and it will not coat a strawberry. Use it on ice cream, fold it into a cake batter, or let it set and chop it into cookie dough.

Overheated chocolate is harder to save. Take it off the heat immediately, tip it into a cool bowl, and stir in a handful of unmelted chopped chocolate to bring the temperature down. If it has actually scorched, the burnt flavour stays and no amount of stirring removes it.

What temperature should chocolate be melted to?

Callebaut publish the standard working figures, and they differ by type because milk solids are less heat tolerant than cocoa solids.

Dark chocolate: melt to 40 to 50C / 113 to 122F. Some traditional curves go to 50 to 55C. Cool to 27C / 81F, then work at 31 to 32C / 88 to 90F, and never above 34.5C / 94F.

Milk chocolate: melt to 45C / 113F, cool to 27C / 81F, work at 29 to 30C / 84 to 86F, and never above 32.5C / 90.5F.

White chocolate: melt to 45C / 113F, cool to 27C / 81F, work at 28 to 29C / 82 to 84F, and never above 31.5C / 88.7F.

Ghirardelli's home kitchen version of the same idea is simpler: heat to 110 to 115F, cool to 95 to 100F, then bring back to 87 to 91F to work with it, and stay under 130F throughout.

If you are only melting chocolate to stir into a batter or drizzle over a traybake, none of the cooling and reheating applies. You only need to stay under the scorching ceiling. The cooling steps are for tempering, which is what gives set chocolate its snap and its shine.

Is a bain-marie better than a microwave?

It is more forgiving and it takes longer. King Arthur timed both: a double boiler over an inch of simmering water took 5 to 6 minutes and scored 2 out of 10 for scorching risk, against 90 seconds and 3 out of 10 in the microwave. Both melted the chocolate properly.

Set a metal or glass bowl over a saucepan holding about an inch of water at a bare simmer, not a boil. The bowl must not touch the water, and it should sit inside the rim of the pan rather than perching on top of it, so steam cannot escape around the edge and condense into the chocolate.

Take the bowl off the heat when about two thirds of the chocolate has melted and stir the rest through. The bowl stays hot for a long time.

Dry the base of the bowl before you pour, because the water clinging to it is exactly the water that seizes the next batch.

The microwave wins for speed and for one less thing to wash. The bain-marie wins if you are melting a large quantity, if you are working with white chocolate, or if you want to hold the chocolate liquid while you dip.

Why did my melted chocolate dry dull and streaky?

Because it was melted rather than tempered. Cocoa butter can crystallise into six different forms, and only one of them, form five, gives a hard set, a clean snap and a gloss. When you simply melt chocolate and let it cool, you get a mixture of forms, and the result is soft, dull, and often streaked with pale grey fat bloom.

Tempering is the process of steering the cocoa butter into that one crystal form, and it is the reason for those cooling and rewarming figures. Melt fully, cool to about 27C to force crystal formation, then warm back to the working temperature to melt off the unstable crystals and leave the good ones as seeds.

The easy home method is seeding. Melt about two thirds of the chocolate to the melting temperature, take it off the heat, and stir in the remaining third chopped very small. The unmelted chocolate is already in form five and carries those crystals into the batch as it dissolves. Callebaut's own version uses 5 per cent callets stirred into chocolate cooled to around 31C for dark and 29C for milk and white.

None of this matters for a chocolate sauce, a ganache, or chocolate going into a cake. It matters for anything that has to set on the outside of something else.
