Chicken internal temperature

Chicken is cooked when the thickest part reaches 74C / 165F on an instant-read thermometer. That is the figure USDA FSIS, the FDA Food Code and Health Canada all give for chicken pieces, and at 165F it is instantaneous: no holding time, no rest required for safety. Probe the thickest part of the breast, thigh or wing, away from bone, fat and gristle, and check every piece separately rather than trusting one. Colour tells you nothing reliable, because safely cooked poultry runs anywhere from white through pink to tan. Dark meat is often taken higher than 165F for texture, which is a separate question from whether it is safe.

Before you cook this.

Chicken is done at 74C / 165F in the thickest part, verified with a thermometer. At 165F the kill step is instantaneous: USDA FSIS requires no hold time and no rest for safety, and the FDA Food Code 2022 lists poultry at 165F for under one second. Nothing on this page recommends a lower endpoint.

Ingredients

Equipment. Instant-read digital thermometer

Method

  1. Find the thickest part of the piece: the deepest point of the breast, the inner thigh, the meaty part of the wing.
  2. Push the probe into that spot, sideways if the piece is thin, so the sensing tip sits in the middle of the meat and not out the other side.
  3. Keep it off bone, fat and gristle. Bone conducts heat differently from muscle and will give you a reading that belongs to the bone.
  4. Wait for the number to settle rather than reading it the instant it appears.
  5. Look for 74C / 165F or higher. Below that, put the chicken back on the heat and check again in a few minutes.
  6. Check each piece separately. A tray of thighs does not cook evenly and the piece at the edge of the pan is not the one to judge the tray by.

Where exactly do you put the thermometer?

USDA FSIS is specific about this: for poultry parts, insert the thermometer into the thickest area and avoid the bone, and keep it off fat and gristle as well. On a whole bird that means the innermost part of the thigh and wing and the thickest part of the breast, all three. On a thin cutlet, go in from the side, because a probe pushed straight down through half an inch of meat has its sensing tip somewhere in the pan.

Health Canada adds the instruction people skip most often: if you are cooking more than one piece, check each piece. Ovens have hot corners, pans have crowded edges, and the thigh you happened to probe is evidence about that thigh only.

Why are pink meat and clear juices unreliable?

FSIS says it plainly: colour is not a doneness indicator, and safely cooked poultry can run from white to pink to tan. The pigment involved is myoglobin, and it does not denature at one fixed temperature. The pH of the muscle moves the goalposts, so meat that sits high on the pH scale can hold a pink cast well past 165F while being entirely cooked.

There is a second cause specific to young birds. Broiler-fryers slaughtered at six to eight weeks have bones that have not finished calcifying, and marrow pigment seeps through into the meat around them, staining it red or purple near the bone. That happens whatever the internal temperature.

Clear juices are the same problem wearing different clothes. The pink in the juice is myoglobin dissolved in water, not blood, and it loses its colour on the same unreliable schedule the meat does. FSIS lists clear juices among the indicators that do not tell you the food is safe.

Do chicken thighs need to go higher than 165F?

Not for safety. 74C / 165F is the floor for every cut, breast and thigh alike. The higher numbers you see attached to dark meat are about texture, and it is worth keeping the two reasons apart.

Dark meat carries a lot of connective tissue, and its collagen dissolves into gelatin over a band running roughly from 140F to 195F. The longer the meat spends in that band, the more of the conversion happens, so thighs and drumsticks get more tender the further past the safety number they go. America's Test Kitchen puts thighs and drumsticks at 175F and says they are better still at 195F. ThermoWorks pulls fried dark meat at 168F and takes wings to 175F for the same reason.

Breast meat has very little collagen, so it gains nothing above the safety number and only loses moisture. Same bird, opposite advice, and the difference is connective tissue.

How much does chicken keep cooking after it leaves the heat?

Enough to matter on large pieces and not much on small ones. ThermoWorks measured it directly: a 245g chicken breast roasted at 425F climbed 12.9F after coming out of the oven, while a 198g piece cooked at 300F rose 7.9F. Carryover scales with the mass of the piece and with how hot you cooked it, so a whole roast bird coasts a long way and a cutlet barely coasts at all.

In practice that means you can pull a big piece a few degrees short of a hot oven and let it arrive on the rack. On a thin cutlet, or anything out of a simmering pot, do not spend the margin you do not have. Take the reading you actually want.

Is the number different for whole birds, ground chicken or stuffing?

USDA holds 165F across the board: pieces, whole birds, ground chicken, and any stuffing cooked inside the bird, which has to reach 165F in its own right rather than inheriting the reading from the meat around it.

Health Canada agrees on pieces and on ground poultry at 74C / 165F, and is stricter on one item. Whole poultry on the Canadian chart is 82C / 180F, measured in the thickest part of the breast. If you are following Canadian guidance, that is the figure for the roast.
