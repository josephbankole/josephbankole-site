Travelport and Mastercard let an AI agent buy a live flight

Joseph Bankole13 November 20263 min read5 sourcesAgentic commerce · Payments

An AI travel agent built by Booked AI searched for, booked, paid for and ticketed a flight on a live airline without the customer leaving the conversation, Travelport announced on Thursday 12 November with Booked AI, Basis Theory and Mastercard [1]. The agent never saw the card number. The release names the airline only as “a major international carrier” [1].

The agent pulled a live New Distribution Capability offer, the fare as the airline itself prices it, through Travelport TripServices. Once the customer confirmed flight and fare in the chat, Mastercard Agent Pay made the payment with a tokenised credential while the real card sat in Basis Theory’s PCI-compliant vault, and the ticket was issued through Travelport’s and the airline’s own systems [1]. The airline remained the merchant throughout, with no intermediary checkout between it and the money [1].

The customer approved the specific booking and amount before payment, and Mastercard’s Verifiable Intent tied that approval to the transaction. Had the details not matched, the payment would not have gone through, and any further purchase needs a fresh approval from the cardholder [1]. For anyone building checkout for agentic commerce, that rule is testable: the payment either matches the booking and amount the customer approved, or it fails. James Armstead, chief technology officer at Basis Theory, made the narrow case for starting with flights: “We’re expecting too much from general-purpose agents right now” [1]. The release describes a single booking and gives no volumes or timetable [1].

Coinbase moves the limits off the agent

Coinbase has added Agent Controls to Coinbase for Agents, its MCP server for researching and placing trades through AI assistants, The Paypers reported on 13 November [2]. Three controls are live: a maximum size per trade set in dollars, a 24-hour volume ceiling above which trades are not executed, and an auto-stop that halts the agent once the portfolio falls past a chosen percentage loss, until the user switches trading back on in the Coinbase app [2]. A fourth, permissions by product category across crypto, equities and derivatives, is marked as coming soon [2].

The limits run on Coinbase’s servers rather than inside the agent, and they can be changed only from the user’s logged-in account behind a password and two-factor authentication, so an agent cannot raise its own ceiling [2]. Coinbase still states that users direct all agent activity and carry the risk of the trades their agents execute [2].

Square lets outside assistants into the seller account

Square made Squaremates, its business agent and the successor to Managerbot, generally available to US sellers on 12 November, and it is now the first thing a seller sees on signing in to Square Dashboard [3]. It arrived with an improved Square MCP server, which lets a seller connect a third-party AI assistant once and have it make changes from a defined set of Square actions, and a new Square CLI that gives developers’ own agents command-line access to Square’s APIs [3]. Squaremates, Square MCP and Square CLI come at no extra cost to US sellers, though Squaremates is not yet available to those on Multi-Account Suite [3].

The purchasing piece is still in beta. A restocking capability checks what is running low, prepares an order and builds it on the supplier’s own website, “ready for the seller to complete” [3]. The agent fills the basket and the seller pays.

Banks build both ends of a stablecoin payment

PNC launched a limited pilot on 9 November that lets eligible corporate and institutional clients receive USDC on Ethereum through Coinbase’s payments products, with each token converted to a dollar and deposited into a designated PNC account, so the client never holds the stablecoin [4]. PNC disclosed no fees, no settlement-time targets and no client numbers [4].

The issuing end is further off. Goldman Sachs, Citi, Santander and Deutsche Bank are among banks committing about $270 million to fund five years of operations for a US-incorporated dollar stablecoin issuer codenamed Project Faro, Bloomberg reported on 10 November, as relayed by Decrypt [5]. The venture grew out of a September pledge by 21 institutions to launch in the first half of 2027; PNC is among them and JPMorgan is not [5]. It has no chief executive and no final name [5].
