Hong Kong gives paying AI agents an identity check, and Wall Street's stablecoin plans cost Circle 3 per cent

Joseph Bankole29 August 20263 min read7 sourcesAgentic commerce · Payments

Hong Kong put a name to the thing every payments team building for agents keeps circling: before an AI agent moves money, somebody has to be able to say whose agent it is. On 27 August 2026 the city’s four financial regulators, working with Cyberport, named the first cohort of the GenA.I. Sandbox++, 36 use cases chosen from nearly 100 proposals, involving 30 financial institutions and 27 technology partners, with agentic AI as the cohort’s focus [1]. The one I would watch is HKT Payment and Red Date Technology’s Agentic ID framework: decentralised identifiers and verifiable credentials that register an agent and bind it to a verified person or business before it initiates payments, wallet top-ups, peer-to-peer transfers or transactions between institutions [2]. Technical trials start later this year on a platform run by Cyberport’s Artificial Intelligence Supercomputing Centre [1].

I have spent this year reading agent-payment schemes from Visa, Mastercard and Google, and each of them answers the identity question with its own registry. What Hong Kong is testing is the version where the regulator convenes the registry, which is the version banks can actually build against without betting on whose directory wins. Know-your-agent is the licensing question, and a sandbox is where licensing questions turn into rules.

Wall Street’s stablecoin, priced by Circle’s shareholders

The Wall Street Journal reported on 26 August that Bank of America, Wells Fargo and Santander are among more than a dozen institutions advancing a global stablecoin venture aimed at commercial clients, a US dollar token first, with the euro and other G7 currencies as the possible next step [3]. JPMorgan Chase has held preliminary internal talks about its own coin; a spokeswoman said “While we have no plans to issue a stablecoin” the bank could review its options as demand and regulation evolve [3]. By Friday the market had done its own arithmetic: Circle and Coinbase shares each fell more than 3 per cent on the reports [4].

The caveat the Journal itself carries is the load-bearing part: no membership list, no governance model, no backing arrangements, no timetable [3]. Thursday’s edition covered the BankChain Alliance, 3,283 smaller banks organising their own chain for 2027. Add this consortium and the pattern holds. The banks are building ahead of the CLARITY Act, which the Senate left unfinished when it adjourned for the August recess; it reconvenes on 14 September [5]. They are building for the rules they expect, and a 3 per cent repricing of the incumbent issuer is what expectation looks like on a ticker.

An agentic commerce alliance, sold by the integrator

Rezolve Ai and Tech Mahindra announced a global alliance on 28 August to deploy agentic commerce across large enterprises: Rezolve’s Brain Commerce for product discovery and Brain Checkout for merchant-controlled transactions, with its TraceWare and Auditable AI layers carrying the audit trail, distributed through Tech Mahindra’s consulting and integration business, which the release sizes at more than 1,100 clients and 146,000 professionals across 90 countries [6] [7]. Every figure in that sentence comes from the companies’ joint release; no customer, deployment or date is named in it.

The tell worth keeping is who is doing the selling. When agentic checkout is being packaged by systems integrators for enterprises with compliance teams, the technology has entered the procurement phase, where an audit trail is a contract term rather than a slide. Hong Kong’s sandbox teaches the same thing from the regulator’s side.

What I would do this week

If your roadmap includes accepting payments from agents, read the HKMA cohort list for what a regulator considers testable today, because the Agentic ID trial is the closest thing yet to a public spec for know-your-agent. If you hold or settle in USDC, the consortium story is your concentration-risk memo writing itself: a bank-issued commercial dollar token would compete for exactly the treasury flows that pay Circle’s bills.

The trials start in Hong Kong later this year. The Senate is back on 14 September. Both calendars now matter more to stablecoin issuers than their own.
